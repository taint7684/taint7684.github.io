HL-OMS: from paper tickets to a real-time ordering system

A Vietnamese restaurant in Sydney CBD loses money and time to handwritten order tickets. I took the project from first meeting to UAT, a working prototype and a pitch.

Role
Business Analyst at a software vendor
Method
4-week discovery + 5 two-week sprints
Solution

The problem

Waiters write orders on paper and walk them to the kitchen. At a Friday peak of 45 orders an hour this produces measurable losses:

3–5wrong or missed dishes per day (~AUD 900/month in remakes)
22 minaverage wait for the first dish at peak
1–2wrong bills per week; 2 near-miss allergy incidents a year

Success looks like: orders reach the right kitchen station instantly, each dish's status is visible in real time, and bills calculate GST and surcharges correctly.

Australian context built into the requirements

Prices include 10% GST · NSW public-holiday surcharge disclosed per ACCC guidance · tax invoices on request (ATO) · allergen information for the 11 declared allergen groups (FSANZ Standard 1.2.3) · data stored in Australia.

Process: as-is to to-be

I marked six numbered pain points on the as-is process, each backed by observation evidence, so the to-be design could show exactly which one it removes.

Requirements

  1. Business needs with baselines7 business needs, each with a measured baseline and a 6-month target — e.g. wrong dishes from 3–5/day to ≤ 1/day.
  2. ElicitationElicitation plan, a structured first meeting with a question log linked to who answers and how, minutes, and decision and change logs.
  3. Stakeholders, risk and NFRsStakeholder analysis, RACI, risk register and 23 measurable NFRs rewritten from vague drafts like "the system must be fast".
  4. User stories40 stories (35 MVP) across 10 epics with 73 acceptance criteria, laid out on a story map by release.

Example: a rule fixed by the use-case exception flow

The allergy rule was first written with AND. The exception flow showed it was really OR — "no allergy declared or the allergy note has been read". I corrected the logic and added a truth table for every rule.

Validation

The prototype runs the business rules, so user testing found business errors, not just layout issues — for example a split bill that was one cent short, which a static wireframe would never reveal.

99UAT test cases
100%pass in round 2 (4 defects found and fixed)
0traceability gaps: 35/35 stories · 73/73 AC · 21/21 rules

The traceability matrix and UAT counts are generated by a script that exits with an error on any gap. I added it after a hand count of high-priority test cases came out wrong (56 instead of 55).

Business value

Year-1 costAUD 49,200
Annual benefitAUD 40,700
Payback~18 months
3-year NPVAUD 45,900

Final deliverables: BRD, SRS, UAT report, user guide, go-live plan, business case and pitch deck.

What I learned

  • A need without a baseline cannot prove value. Version 1 of the BACCM had qualitative needs only; version 1.1 split them into seven needs with numbers.
  • Pure Scrum does not fit a client who signs fixed scope and budget. A hybrid — discovery to lock the BRD/SRS, then sprints — matched how the client buys.
  • Anything counted by hand will eventually be wrong. Generating the RTM and test counts from source removed a whole class of errors.
